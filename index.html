Cyprus is hard to fit neatly into a postcard. It is one of the oldest continuously inhabited places on Earth, the mythological birthplace of Aphrodite, and a meeting point of three continents. What follows is the pitch, and the places we would actually send you.

9,000 years of history

At Khirokitia, about 40 minutes from Larnaca, you can walk through a Neolithic settlement that was founded around 7,000 BC. To put that in perspective: these circular stone houses were already ancient ruins by the time anyone thought to build Stonehenge or the Pyramids. Reconstructed dwellings let you step inside and see how people actually lived nine millennia ago.

Where to point the car: Kourion, west of Limassol, is the south’s best site. A Greco-Roman theatre hangs on a sea cliff, the mosaics are still in the floors they were laid in, and the Earthquake House preserves the morning in AD 365 when one of the strongest earthquakes in Mediterranean history flattened the city mid-breakfast. Go late in the afternoon, when the light turns and the coaches leave. Amathus, on the other side of Limassol, is the opposite experience: the ruins of an entire ancient kingdom, usually empty. Its four-ton stone jar sits in the Louvre; the site itself you may have to yourself.

In Nicosia, the Cyprus Museum packs nine millennia into a building from 1908, including the two thousand terracotta figures found at Agia Irini standing in a circle, exactly as they were left. The collection moves to a new building, probably in 2027, so see the old one while it exists. The Leventis Museum nearby is free and tells the story of the city itself; do it before you walk the Green Line.

Where the heroes of the Iliad started over

After the fall of Troy, the heroes of the Iliad scattered across the Mediterranean, and an unusual number of them ended up in Cyprus. Teucer, the great archer who fought alongside his half-brother Ajax, was banished by his own father for failing to bring Ajax's body home. He sailed to Cyprus and founded the city of Salamis on the eastern coast, naming it after the island he could never return to. Agapenor, leader of the Arcadian forces at Troy, was blown off course by a storm and landed on the western shore, where he founded Paphos and built the sanctuary of Aphrodite.

Legend says that Aphrodite herself was born from the sea foam at Petra tou Romiou in Paphos. The Sanctuary of Aphrodite, built by the Mycenaeans in the 12th century BC, drew pilgrims from across the ancient world for over a thousand years until a Roman emperor shut it down in 391 AD.

At the Tombs of the Kings in Paphos, a vast underground necropolis carved directly into the rock, the island's elite were buried from the 4th century BC in chambers designed to look like actual houses, with columns and frescoed interiors. You can climb down into them. The Roman villa mosaics at Kato Paphos depict mythological scenes in remarkable detail.

One honest note: Petra tou Romiou is a fine rock with a great story, but it is a stop, not a destination. Pull over, take the photo, keep driving.

The Akamas Peninsula

The Akamas Peninsula, on the island's northwest tip, is one of the last unspoiled natural regions in the Mediterranean. There are no paved roads through its interior. Over 600 plant species thrive here, including dozens that are endemic to Cyprus and grow nowhere else on Earth (among them the Akamas tulip, whose entire known world population consists of roughly 200 plants). It is home to sea turtles, griffon vultures, fruit bats in limestone caves (famously filmed by David Attenborough), and a landscape that shifts from sandy coves to pine forests to mountain scrub within a few kilometers.

The Avakas Gorge, on the southern edge of the peninsula, is a canyon carved through limestone over millions of years by the Avgas River. The walls rise up to 30 meters and narrow to just 2 or 3 meters at the tightest point, with a massive boulder suspended between the cliffs above you.

And the Blue Lagoon at Akamas is exactly as beautiful as every photo you will find on Google.

If you walk one trail in Cyprus, walk the Adonis and Aphrodite loop from the Baths of Aphrodite: nine and a half kilometers climbing to a ridge above the Blue Lagoon coast. Do Adonis first, so the descent hands you the sea the whole way down. Start early; there is no shade and no reliable water. Lara Bay, on the peninsula’s west side, is the turtle beach: loggerheads and greens nest in June and July, the hatchlings are gone by the end of September, and the road is unpaved on purpose. Bring everything, leave nothing, and keep lights off after dark in season.

A piece of the ocean floor, 2,000 meters in the air

Aristotelis became obsessed with rocks after a geology course.

The Troodos range in the center of the island looks like ordinary mountain scenery. It is not. It is a piece of the ocean floor. Geologists call it an ophiolite: a fragment of oceanic crust and upper mantle that formed 92 million years ago at the bottom of the ancient Tethys Ocean, then was pushed upward by the collision of the African and Eurasian plates until it became the spine of Cyprus.

It is considered the most complete and best-preserved ophiolite sequence in the world. You can literally walk from rocks that were once the Earth's upper mantle to what was once the ocean floor, all on hiking trails. The Troodos Geopark covers over 1,100 square kilometers with 38 geosites and 84 trails. The visitor center, housed in a restored early 20th-century school at the site of a rehabilitated asbestos mine, is one of the best small geology museums anywhere.

The region is also home to one of the largest concentrations of Byzantine churches and monasteries outside of Greece, and to the wine villages (those get their own section below).

Two walks put the geology under your feet. The Artemis trail circles the summit cone of Olympus at 1,850 meters, nearly flat, with the whole island below; bring a layer even in October. Better still is Teisia tis Madaris: a three-and-a-half kilometer geo-trail around the dyke walls of the Madari ridge with more views per kilometer than hikes ten times its length, and a fire lookout at the top if you extend it. On autumn mornings you are often above the clouds.

Abandoned mines and blood-red lakes

The word "copper" comes from Cyprus. The Latin cuprum derives from aes Cyprium, "metal of Cyprus," and the island supplied nearly all of the Roman Empire's copper at the height of its power. Mining eventually collapsed, lay dormant for centuries, was revived by the British, and then collapsed again.

What remains are surreal hiking landscapes. Near the village of Mitsero, about 30 minutes from Nicosia, abandoned open-pit copper mines have filled with groundwater that turned deep, vivid red from the concentration of heavy metals left behind. The locals call it the Red Lake. Its color shifts with the seasons and even the time of day. You cannot swim in it (the water is toxic). Nearby, the Kokkinogia mine still has its rusting machinery and a horizontal tunnel you can peer into. Further along the same route, the mines around Mathiatis and the village of Sha offer more colored lakes and gossan rock formations in yellow, brown, and orange. All part of the Troodos Geopark.

Stone bridges, ancient churches, and things you find by accident

Deep in the Paphos Forest, the Tzelefos Bridge is a single-arched Venetian stone bridge from the 16th century, the largest on the island, once part of the camel road network built to carry copper from the Troodos mines to the sea. Its humpback design let camels cross over it when the rivers ran high and walk under it through the dry riverbed in summer. There are 27 Venetian bridges designated as ancient monuments across Cyprus, and you can hike a trail connecting three of them through the forest.

In Larnaca, the Church of Saint Lazarus was built in the 9th century directly over the tomb of the biblical Lazarus, who (according to Orthodox tradition) fled to Cyprus after being raised from the dead, became the city's first bishop, and lived another 30 years. You can walk down into the crypt and see the empty sarcophagus. Near Kiti, the Angeloktisti Church (its name means "built by angels") preserves a 6th-century mosaic of the Virgin and Child that rivals anything in Ravenna. Mosaics from this period exist in only two places on Earth: here and Mount Sinai.

But beyond the headline sites, small Byzantine and medieval chapels are scattered across the island, tucked into valleys, perched on hillsides, hiding inside villages. Ten of the painted churches in Troodos are collectively UNESCO World Heritage listed, with elaborate murals inside modest, barn-roofed exteriors that were built to be inconspicuous. There are many more that aren't in any guidebook. You just find them.

The crossroads of everything

Cyprus sits closer to Beirut than to Athens, closer to Damascus than to Rome. For most of recorded history, this positioning made it one of the most strategically contested pieces of land on Earth, and everyone who ever built an empire in this part of the world eventually wanted it.

The list of rulers reads like a graduate seminar syllabus: Mycenaean Greeks, Assyrians, Egyptians, Persians, Alexander the Great, the Ptolemaic dynasty, the Roman Empire, the Byzantines, Richard the Lionheart (who conquered it on his way to the Third Crusade and then sold it), the Lusignan dynasty, the Venetians, the Ottoman Empire, and the British. Each of them left something behind, and you can see most of it.

Nicosia, the capital, has been continuously inhabited for over 5,500 years and is the last divided capital in the world. In 1963, a British general drew a line across the city with a green pencil to stop intercommunal fighting. In 1974, following a Greek-backed coup and a Turkish military response, that line became a UN-controlled buffer zone stretching 180 kilometers across the entire island. The old city, enclosed within 16th-century Venetian walls, is still split. You can walk through a checkpoint on Ledra Street, show your passport twice within 15 meters, and cross from the Republic of Cyprus into Northern Cyprus. On one side, signs are in Greek. On the other, Turkish. Between them, a narrow strip of crumbling buildings, barbed wire, and UN observation posts.

The walk that makes it concrete: start at the Leventis Museum for the story, follow the dead-end streets west along the barricades, and cross at Ledra Street (passport, a few minutes). On the other side, the Büyük Han is a restored Ottoman caravanserai full of workshops and coffee, and the Selimiye Mosque is the oldest great Gothic building on the island, wearing minarets. Come back past the Ledra Palace crossing and have coffee at the Home for Cooperation, a café that operates physically inside the buffer zone. Do not photograph the checkpoints or anyone in uniform.

The old town also feeds you properly. Our own row: Μπéμπα for modern Cypriot cooking, Topika for meze, Rokoko and γρανáζι for the bar end of the evening. The OXI market sets up on the Venetian walls on Wednesday and Saturday mornings; go before ten. Loukoumades Kyrillis has been frying the same thing since 1965 and needs no menu. And the Hamam Omerye has been a bathhouse, on and off, since the 14th century: book a treatment, and if the call to prayer from the mosque next door reaches you under the dome, that is the moment you will remember.

The other side

You can cross. Take your passport, walk through Ledra Street, or drive through Agios Dometios for the Kyrenia side and Deryneia for Famagusta. Buy the mandatory insurance at the vehicle crossing and check whether your rental contract allows it; if it does not, park at Deryneia and take a taxi across. The one rule that matters: arrive on the island through the south. Landing at Ercan counts as illegal entry into the Republic and can cause you real problems at the crossings.

Famagusta is the most layered single town on the island: Venetian walls that survived one of history’s great sieges, and a French Gothic cathedral, where Lusignan kings were crowned Kings of Jerusalem, that has been a mosque since 1571. Next to it lies Varosha, the resort suburb sealed since 1974 and partly reopened as a fenced corridor. It is the most photographed ghost town in the Mediterranean, and it is also tens of thousands of people’s confiscated homes; some of the displaced visit, some refuse to. Go quietly, off season, on foot, after the living old town, and the contrast will tell you more about this island than any museum.

Ancient Salamis, just north, is where the gymnasium colonnade stands against the sea: unsigned, overgrown, and the most evocative ruin in Cyprus precisely because nobody is managing your experience there. St Hilarion, above Kyrenia, is the best view on the island from a Crusader castle; the climb is real, and the opening hours obey the army, not the website. Pair it with Bellapais Abbey, where Lawrence Durrell wrote, and the 2,300-year-old shipwreck in Kyrenia castle.

The animals

Cyprus’s wildlife is a story of comebacks and near misses. The griffon vulture fell to a few dozen birds here; a rescue project has been flying in Spanish vultures for years, and the colony at Kensington Cliffs near Episkopi is now the one place on the island to watch them. Go mid-morning, when the thermals build. It is free, and there will be nobody there.

The mouflon, the wild sheep on the coins and the airline tails, roams the Paphos Forest in the thousands and is almost never seen. The enclosure at Stavros tis Psokas is the near-guaranteed option, but it closed as a precaution in March 2026; check it has reopened before committing to the long forest drive. The flamingos arrive on the Larnaca and Akrotiri salt lakes in November and leave by March, ten thousand strong in a good winter. In summer the lakes are a blinding salt crust, striking in its own way.

The strangest story: the Akamas caves hold Europe’s only fruit bat. David Attenborough filmed the colonies, and his notes later guided the researchers who mapped the roosts. For much of the twentieth century the government classified the bats as pests and fumigated their caves; the colonies that survive are recovering, which is why nobody publishes where they are.

If you are a birder, the Akrotiri peninsula is the island’s richest site, and early October brings the raptor migration, with red-footed falcons over the Mandria fields. BirdLife Cyprus publishes weekly sightings; trust those over promises.

Diving a top-ten wreck

If you are a diver (or have ever been curious about it), Cyprus has one of the longest diving seasons in the Mediterranean, stretching from March through November.

The Zenobia is a 172-meter Swedish ferry that sank off Larnaca in 1980 on its maiden voyage, taking over 100 trucks with it. It now rests on its side at 42 meters depth. You can still see the trucks chained inside the cargo decks, the massive propellers, the bridge, even the cafeteria with its coffee machine still attached to the bar. Marine life has turned the wreck into a thriving reef: barracuda, grouper, loggerhead turtles, and lionfish are all regulars.

Not a diver? Sea kayaking out of Cape Greco runs until the end of October: three hours along the cliffs to the sea caves, with swimming stops in water that holds 24 degrees deep into autumn.

The beaches

Nissi Beach in Ayia Napa is the one you have probably seen in photos: white sand, shallow turquoise water, and a small natural bridge connecting to a tiny island you can wade to. Fig Tree Bay in Protaras regularly appears on lists of Europe's best beaches. And Lara Bay, tucked inside the Akamas Peninsula, is a protected turtle nesting beach that feels untouched.

The food

We will let you discover most of this one for yourselves. But if you leave Cyprus without having had a proper meze (think 15 to 25 small dishes served one after another), fresh halloumi that was made that morning, slow-cooked kleftiko lamb, and loukoumades drizzled with honey, Aristotelis will take it personally.

Where we actually eat: Hercules in Zygi for fish (order the calamari), with Xefoto, whose own boats catch what it serves, as the second opinion in the same village. To souvlaki tou Soukri in Paralimni, the souvlaki worth a detour. Adamos in Lefkara, which is the answer locals give when you ask in the village. In the Troodos, Katoi in Omodos does kleftiko from a proper clay oven and justifies braving the island’s most coached village; in Kakopetria, To Anoi is the local answer, while the famous Mill serves real trout at famous-Mill prices.

And the single most real food experience on the island is lunch at Sofia and Andreas’ house in Letymbou: halloumi made in a vat in front of you, bread from the stone oven, then lunch at their family table. Book ahead, come hungry, cancel your dinner plans.

The wine

Cyprus has been making wine for about as long as anyone; Commandaria, the amber dessert wine of the Krasochoria foothills, is plausibly the oldest wine still made under its own name, and the Knights Hospitaller ran it as an export business from Kolossi castle seven centuries ago. (The castle itself is a 25-minute stop on the way to Kourion; go for the story, not the tower.)

The wine villages reward a full day. Zambartas in Agios Amvrosios gives the best tasting on the island and is busy reviving the indigenous grapes: Xynisteri, Maratheftiko, Yiannoudi. Vlassides in Kilani makes the reference Shiraz in a building worth the trip by itself, and Kilani is the village to eat in (not Omodos). Kyperounda grows Xynisteri at 1,400 meters, about as high as European vineyards go, and pairs naturally with the Madari ridge walk above it. Tsiakkas in Pelendri bridges the dry wines and Commandaria from a tasting terrace over the valley. For Commandaria itself, skip the supermarket bottles and visit Revecca in Agios Mamas, a tiny organic family producer that pours it in the old family house.

Slowing down

Cyprus does luxury best when it is woven into a village. Casale Panayiotis in Kalopanayiotis is the proof: a hundred years of stone houses restored across a Marathasa valley village, a spa whose treatments people rank among the best of their lives, and a working monastery across the river. The lanes are steep; pack accordingly. Apokryfo in Lofou does the same trick at boutique scale in an unspoilt wine village, fifteen minutes from Vlassides and Zambartas.

Of the big resorts, Anassa on the edge of the Akamas is the one whose service earns the price; eat half your dinners at the Latchi fish quay five minutes away and you have fixed its one weakness. Amara in Limassol is the modern option that guests rate above its flashier neighbours, and its restaurants, Matsuhisa and Locatelli, are destination meals on their own. We would skip the Parklane, and the once-charming Library Hotel in Kalavasos has faded. In Nicosia, the Hamam Omerye (see the crossroads, above) is pampering at the price of a dinner.
